MLB Pitcher Arsenal Analysis ~ Russell Petersen

This project uses 2025 MLB data of 125 starting pitchers from the website Baseball Savant to investigate how a pitcher's pitch arsenal — the types and mix of pitches they throw — affects their performance metrics. By analyzing pitch movement profiles and categorizing pitchers based on their pitch usage patterns, this analysis reveals which combinations of pitches and usage rates are most effective for achieving better outcomes on the mound.

Pitcher Categories

Categorization Framework: To better understand how different pitching styles impact performance, pitchers were categorized into distinct groups based on their pitch usage rates and types. The primary classifications include fastball-dominant pitchers, those with balanced arsenals, and pitchers who rely heavily on offspeed and breaking pitches. This framework helps identify patterns in how successful pitchers construct their arsenals.

Classification Process: Pitchers were then assigned to a group, with a specific order of evaluation to ensure accurate classification. For instance, pitchers with four-seam fastball usage above 40% were classified as fastball-dominant, while those with four-seam usage below 25% were considered less fastball-dependent. The visualization below shows how different pitcher categories distribute across the strike zone, highlighting the strategic differences in how various pitcher types approach batters.

Results

Performance Metrics Overview: This is how each pitch arsenal performed across key statistical categories: Expected Weighted On-Base Average (xWOBA), Strikeout to Walk Percentage (K-BB%), Whiff Rate (Whiff%), and Average Exit Velocity. These metrics collectively provide a comprehensive view of pitcher effectiveness.

xWOBA: Measures the quality of contact a pitcher allows; a lower xWOBA indicates they are limiting hitters to weaker outcomes. This is one of the most predictive offensive metrics available.
K-BB%: Reflects a pitcher's command and swing-and-miss ability, with higher percentages being more desirable as they show the pitcher is recording strikeouts while minimizing walks. This demonstrates overall pitcher control.
Whiff%: Directly quantifies how often batters swing and miss at pitches, highlighting a pitcher's raw stuff and ability to induce poor contact. Higher whiff rates generally correlate with strikeout success.
Average Exit Velocity: Indicates the average speed of batted balls against a pitcher; lower values suggest they induce weak contact. This metric is particularly important as exit velocity has a direct correlation with batting average and home run probability.

Summary

Predicting and analyzing success in Major League Baseball is always going to be a difficult task, which is why organizations spend millions annually trying to hire the best minds to evaluate players. This project attempts to identify patterns in pitch arsenal construction that correlate with superior pitching performance.

Pitchers who throw an even usage of a lot of different pitchers landed in the middle of all of the performance statistics. This makes sense as different pitches typically generate different results, and having a balanced approach doesn't maximize any particular strength.
By far and away the most successful group was the four seam changeup/splitter group that threw their fastballs at a higher than 25% usage rate, and their offspeed at a higher than 15% usage rate. This combination appears to provide the best balance between fastball effectiveness and offspeed deception.
Heavy fast ball usage groups had some of the lowest whiff rates, which makes sense as these pitches tend to have the least amount of induced movement. What was surprising however was that they still maintained relatively competitive strikeout rates, suggesting that command and location may compensate for reduced movement.

Considerations

This project took a look into starting pitcher data from the 2025 MLB season. Had this analysis included all qualified relief pitchers as well, the data likely would have told a different story, as relief pitchers often specialize in one or two pitches thrown at maximum velocity. Additionally, sample size matters significantly in baseball analysis — some pitchers may have limited batted ball data that makes their metrics less stable. Future analysis could incorporate more seasons of data to identify consistent patterns and reduce the impact of single-season variance.
